# One-Confounder/One-Modifier Result

## Run

The completed run used five honest outer folds, a 30,000-feature word
unigram/bigram/trigram vocabulary inside each outer-training fold, 30
stratified stability subsamples, seven nuisance views for sign-stability
diagnostics, and 400-tree residualized causal forests.

The primary nuisances were an honest cross-fitted logistic stack of the six
BoW and one HTR nuisance views already generated by the multi-model stage-1
run.  Mean outer-training diagnostics were:

| Metric | Mean |
|---|---:|
| Treatment AUROC | 0.838 |
| Treatment Brier | 0.163 |
| Outcome AUROC | 0.822 |
| Outcome Brier | 0.171 |
| Residual constant-effect estimate | 0.125 |

## Did the cohort contrast find PD-L1?

Yes, although the ranking also contained substantial noise.  The most stable
PD-L1-related directions across all five folds were:

### Lower-effect direction

| N-gram | Mean standardized score | Best fold rank |
|---|---:|---:|
| `% low` | -3.73 | 1 |
| `pd-l1 <1` | -3.35 | 5 |
| `low pd-l1 expression` | -3.21 | 10 |
| `pd-l1 <1 %.` | -3.04 | 21 |
| `<1` | -2.95 | 27 |
| `1 % low` | -2.89 | 38 |

### Higher-effect direction

| N-gram | Mean standardized score | Best fold rank |
|---|---:|---:|
| `high pd-l1 expression` | 3.23 | 4 |
| `high pd-l1` | 3.19 | 11 |
| `tps >=` | 2.87 | 21 |
| `pd-l1 tps >=` | 2.37 | 170 |
| `high expression` | 2.08 | 359 |
| `% high` | 2.07 | 764 |
| `tps 60` | 2.01 | 581 |
| `>= 50 %` | 1.98 | 527 |

The signs are correct for the simulated modifier: low/negative PD-L1 language
points toward lower treatment effect, while high PD-L1 language points toward
higher effect.  These scores were constructed without using the oracle PD-L1
or ITE columns.

Several unrelated phrases were also highly stable, including
`syndromes --- physical`, `neurologic symptoms or`, `lung no`, `55.`,
`hour <new note>`, `kidneys`, and `superior vena cava`.  The contrast therefore
narrows the vocabulary and recovers the right concept, but it does not by
itself cleanly separate the true modifier from cohort-specific correlates.

## Causal-forest performance

| Selected X n-grams | PEHE | True/predicted ITE correlation | High-PD-L1 AUROC | PD-L1 gap recovered | Test R-loss improvement vs constant |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 25 | 0.210 | 0.139 | 0.637 | 28.9% | -0.00183 |
| 50 | 0.194 | 0.154 | 0.642 | 24.2% | -0.00099 |
| 100 | 0.185 | 0.119 | 0.636 | 18.9% | -0.00087 |
| 200 | 0.181 | 0.104 | 0.622 | 13.4% | +0.00028 |
| 400 | 0.179 | 0.098 | 0.610 | 9.1% | -0.00024 |

For comparison, the earlier unfiltered 10,000-feature TF-IDF forest had
high-PD-L1 AUROC 0.495 and recovered essentially none of the high-versus-low
PD-L1 effect gap.  The cohort contrast therefore improved modifier-focused
screening, particularly with 25-50 selected features.

It did **not** produce a generally good CATE estimator.  Only the 200-feature
forest improved test R-loss over the constant-effect residual baseline, and
that improvement was tiny.  Predicted ATEs remained high (about 0.12-0.13
versus the true 0.063), indicating remaining nuisance/residual bias.  More
features reduced PEHE largely by shrinking heterogeneity toward a biased
constant, while fewer features better separated the PD-L1 groups but produced
noisier individual estimates.

## Interpretation

The one-off supports the screening hypothesis more than the final-model
hypothesis:

1. Pooling the orthogonal evidence at cohort level can surface the correct
   positive and negative lexical representations of PD-L1 without fitting a
   patient-level R target.
2. Stability weighting does not eliminate many correlated or accidental text
   patterns.
3. Passing the screened terms directly to a causal forest improves PD-L1
   discrimination over the unfiltered TF-IDF forest, but current nuisance
   accuracy and finite-sample noise still prevent reliable CATE estimation.

The next build-out, if pursued, should focus on consolidating correlated
n-grams into term groups and on stronger/calibrated nuisances before adding a
more elaborate final effect model.
