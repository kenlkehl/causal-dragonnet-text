Weighted R-loss versus unweighted pseudo-target regression
=========================================================

The R-learner starts from a residualized treatment-effect equation:

    Y - m(X) = tau(X) * (T - e(X)) + noise

where m(X) is the nuisance outcome model and e(X) is the nuisance treatment
model. The usual R-learner objective fits tau(X) by minimizing:

    sum_i [(Y_i - m_hat_i) - tau(X_i) * (T_i - e_hat_i)]^2

This objective can be rewritten as pseudo-target regression. Define:

    pseudo_tau_i = (Y_i - m_hat_i) / (T_i - e_hat_i)
    weight_i = (T_i - e_hat_i)^2

Then the same objective is:

    sum_i weight_i * [pseudo_tau_i - tau(X_i)]^2

So the treatment-residual weight is not an arbitrary heuristic. It is the
algebraically equivalent form of the residualized causal loss.

The theoretical advantage of the weighted R-loss is that it respects the amount
of causal information in each observation. If T_i is close to e_hat_i, then the
residualized treatment is small. The pseudo-target divides by that small number,
so it can become a very noisy label. The R weight downweights exactly those
unstable labels. Rows with larger absolute treatment residuals contain more
information about the treatment-effect slope and get more influence.

This weighting also preserves the R-learner's orthogonal structure. The target
loss is built from residualized outcome and residualized treatment. That gives
the estimator some robustness to imperfect nuisance models, because first-order
nuisance errors tend to cancel rather than directly biasing tau. Fully
unweighted pseudo-target regression changes the objective. It is no longer
fitting the original residualized equation; it is fitting the noisy ratio labels
as if every ratio were equally reliable.

The practical problem is that the clean theory can be conservative in small,
binary, stochastic settings. Binary outcomes and binary treatments create large
irreducible noise. The pseudo-target can contain a visible subgroup contrast,
but the weighted and regularized learner may still prefer a nearly global tau
because the subgroup contrast explains only a small fraction of total
residualized outcome variation. In our synthetic NSCLC examples, the high PD-L1
group can have a clearly higher mean pseudo-target, while a weighted sparse
R-stage still compresses predictions toward a flat treatment effect.

That does not necessarily mean the R-loss is wrong. It means it is optimizing a
different and more conservative criterion: fit the residualized causal equation
while discounting observations whose treatment residual makes their pseudo-label
unstable. If the subgroup signal is weak relative to stochastic treatment and
outcome variation, this weighting can make the treatment-effect heterogeneity
harder to recover.

Unweighted pseudo-target regression makes the opposite tradeoff. It may be more
sensitive to visible subgroup differences, because every pseudo-label receives
equal weight. That can be useful diagnostically: if unweighted regression finds
PD-L1 but weighted R-loss does not, the representation probably contains the
effect-modifier signal and the bottleneck is the R objective/variance tradeoff.
But unweighted regression also gives too much authority to high-variance ratio
labels, especially when |T - e_hat| is small, and it loses some of the formal
orthogonality that motivates the R-learner.

A useful middle ground is to run a sensitivity sweep:

    1. weighted R-loss: weight = (T - e_hat)^2
    2. clipped weights: weight = max((T - e_hat)^2, floor)
    3. square-root weights: weight = |T - e_hat|
    4. unweighted pseudo-target regression
    5. oracle or group-balanced weighting for diagnostics

If only the unweighted or clipped variants recover the known modifier, then the
experiment is telling us that the modifier signal exists but is too weak under
the strict weighted R objective. That is a useful scientific finding, not just a
modeling failure.

For production causal estimation, the weighted R-loss remains the more
principled target. For discovery and debugging in noisy synthetic binary
experiments, unweighted or softened-weight pseudo-target regression can be a
valuable lens for seeing heterogeneity that the orthodox objective may wash out.

Feature discovery versus final causal estimation
------------------------------------------------

The case for the full weighted R-loss depends on how the R-stage output is being
used. If tau(X) from the R-stage is the final CATE estimator, then the weighted
objective has the stronger theoretical argument. The goal is not just to find
interesting variables; it is to estimate treatment effects under the residualized
causal moment. In that setting, preserving the original R-loss, downweighting
unstable ratio labels, and retaining orthogonality are central.

If the R-stage is only being used to discover candidate variables, the objective
changes. The discovery question is closer to:

    Which pre-treatment text-derived variables might be related to treatment
    effect heterogeneity?

For that purpose, strict adherence to the weighted R-loss is less essential.
The R-stage is acting as a screening instrument, not the final causal estimator.
High-recall discovery may be more valuable than low-variance tau prediction. If
an unweighted pseudo-target makes PD-L1 or another known modifier surface
clearly, while the weighted R-loss suppresses it, the unweighted version may be
the better discovery tool even though it is the less principled final estimator.

This reframes the role of the noisy pseudo-target. In final estimation, noisy
ratio labels are a liability because they directly affect the treatment-effect
estimate. In feature discovery, noisy labels are still a liability, but the
downstream process can filter candidates: an agent proposes explicit variables,
the extraction step turns them into patient-level columns, and the final causal
forest estimates CATE using those extracted variables. The pseudo-target model
does not have to be trusted as the final causal model; it only has to surface
plausible variables for later evaluation.

The risk is false positives. Unweighted pseudo-target regression can elevate
template artifacts, names, documentation style, or variables associated with
nuisance-model errors rather than true effect modification. That is why the
feature-discovery path should save its feature-importance evidence, agent
proposals, extraction coverage, and final forest diagnostics. The researcher can
then distinguish clinically meaningful candidate variables from textual
artifacts.

In short:

    final CATE estimation: weighted R-loss is the cleaner target
    feature discovery: unweighted or softened pseudo-target regression can be
    defensible because sensitivity may matter more than formal optimality

This is the motivation for a multi-model agentic forest path that uses simple
bag-of-words nuisance models and an unweighted pseudo-target model for variable
discovery, then delegates the actual CATE estimation to an explicit-variable
causal forest.
